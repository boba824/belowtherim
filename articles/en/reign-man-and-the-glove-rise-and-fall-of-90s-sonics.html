Rain, Rims, and Broken Promises: The Rise and Fall of Shawn Kemp and Gary Payton in Seattle

Long before modern basketball coined the phrase “Lob City,” two young titans in the rain-soaked Pacific Northwest set the NBA ablaze. Gary Payton and Shawn Kemp formed the most electrifying, terrifying duo of the 1990s—until a single baffling front-office blunder shattered a dynasty in the making.

In the early 1990s, the city of Seattle became the epicenter of a cultural revolution. Out of damp basement garages, grunge bands like Nirvana, Pearl Jam, and Soundgarden reshaped rock music with raw, unfiltered distortion. Just a few blocks away beneath the steel rafters of the Seattle Center Coliseum—later rechristened KeyArena—that exact same rebellious, untamed voltage was being unleashed on the basketball court.

The conductor was an unrelenting, trash-talking point guard forged on the asphalt playgrounds of Oakland: Gary Payton, universally known as “The Glove.” His partner in crime was a 6-foot-10 force of nature who entered the league without playing a single minute of college basketball: Shawn Kemp, the “Reign Man.” Together, they didn’t merely run the fast break—they summoned a thunderstorm.

“The very first time I saw Shawn play at a summer pro-am, only one thought crossed my mind: ‘I gotta play with this guy,’” Gary Payton reflected years later. “He had springs in his legs unlike anything I had ever witnessed. All I had to do was throw the ball somewhere near the square on the backboard, and he would take care of gravity.”

The Birth of the “Sonic Boom” and Rim-Rattling Chaos

Shawn Kemp arrived in Seattle in 1989 as a raw 19-year-old prodigy, and a year later the Sonics used the second overall pick to draft Gary Payton. Their chemistry was virtually telepathic. When head coach George Karl took over midway through the 1991–92 campaign, he weaponized their athleticism into the most suffocating full-court trapping defense the NBA had ever seen.

The formula was simple yet unstoppable: Payton, Nate McMillan, and Seattle’s perimeter hounds would trap opposing ball-handlers near midcourt, force a panicked turnover, and ignite the break. Broadcaster Kevin Calabro’s signature call would echo through the arena as Payton lofted a pass into the rafters and Kemp rocketed through the lane.

Kemp didn’t just dunk the basketball; he sought to dismantle the rim and demoralize anyone foolish enough to contest him. During the 1992 playoffs against the Golden State Warriors, he delivered one of the most iconic poster dunks in league history—immortalized as the “Lister Blister.” Taking off from inside the dotted circle, Kemp contorted his body in midair, detonated over 7-foot center Alton Lister, and crouched at the baseline pointing both index fingers directly into the fallen veteran’s face.

For head coach George Karl, coaching his two mercurial superstars was equal parts exhilarating and maddening.

“Gary, Shawn, and I had different ideas about the right way to play,” Karl wrote in his memoir, Furious George. “Sometimes they decided that a simple fast-break layup or dunk was just too boring. Gary would pass up his easy shot in favor of an alley-oop or bounce the ball off the backboard for Shawn to swoop in and jam it. The crowd would go nuts. Or I would. I could hardly think of a better example of disrespecting the game and the opponent—yet nobody could stop them.”

Playoff Heartbreak and the 1996 Finals Peak

Despite dominating the regular season, the Sonics spent the early 1990s haunted by postseason ghosts. In 1993, they came within one game of the NBA Finals before falling to Charles Barkley and the Phoenix Suns in Game 7 of the Western Conference Finals. A year later, in 1994, Seattle suffered the most agonizing collapse in franchise history: after winning a league-best 63 games, the top-seeded Sonics were stunned 3–2 in the first round by the eighth-seeded Denver Nuggets—the first time an No. 8 seed ever eliminated a No. 1 seed.

Rather than fracturing the duo, the humiliation hardened them. By the 1995–96 season, Payton and Kemp were operating at the absolute peak of their powers. Payton captured NBA Defensive Player of the Year honors, while Kemp averaged a career-high 19.6 points and 11.4 rebounds per game. Seattle tore through the West with a franchise-record 64–18 mark, outlasted the Utah Jazz in a grueling seven-game Western Conference Finals, and reached the NBA Finals for the first time since 1979.

Waiting for them were Michael Jordan and the historic 72–10 Chicago Bulls. After falling into a 3–0 series hole, George Karl finally relented in Game 4 and allowed Payton to guard Jordan full-court. Behind Payton’s relentless ball pressure and Kemp’s Finals averages of 23.3 points and 10.0 rebounds on 55 percent shooting, Seattle stormed back to win Games 4 and 5 before bowing out in Game 6 in Chicago.

“Shawn was an absolute monster in that Finals series,” Payton recalled. “A lot of people believed that if we had pulled off the comeback, Shawn would have been Finals MVP. We were 26 and 27 years old. Walking off the floor in Chicago after Game 6, we were 100 percent convinced the next five years belonged to us.”

The $33 Million Mistake: The Jim McIlvaine Affair

The summer of 1996 should have been about fine-tuning a championship contender. Instead, Sonics general manager Wally Walker authored one of the most catastrophic self-inflicted wounds in sports history.

Years earlier, a young Shawn Kemp had signed a long-term contract that left him severely underpaid as salaries skyrocketed across the league. During the 1995–96 Finals run, Kemp earned just $3.3 million—roughly eighth on the Sonics’ own payroll—despite being a perennial All-NBA selection. Under the league’s new Collective Bargaining Agreement, Seattle could not officially renegotiate his deal until October 1997, though management had given Kemp assurances he would be taken care of.

Then came July 1996. Convinced they needed a shot-blocking center to counter Shaquille O’Neal, the Sonics signed Washington Bullets backup center Jim McIlvaine—a career reserve who had averaged 2.3 points and 2.9 rebounds the previous season—to a jaw-dropping seven-year, $33.6 million contract. Overnight, a player who averaged 15 minutes a night was making significantly more money than Shawn Kemp.

“I didn’t have a problem with McIlvaine personally, but the deal that they gave him was garbage,” Kemp said candidly. “Don’t spend six million a year and bring back something worth 25 bucks. It gave me a very clear sense that the organization wasn’t really committed to me and didn’t respect what I had sacrificed for Seattle.”

The betrayal poisoned the locker room. Kemp held out for the first three weeks of training camp in 1996, and while the Sonics still won 57 games and pushed the Houston Rockets to seven games in the 1997 playoffs, the joy had evaporated. Feeling alienated by management, Kemp demanded a trade and vowed never to wear a Seattle uniform again.

The Fallout and Lingering Regret

In September 1997, the Sonics traded Shawn Kemp to the Cleveland Cavaliers in a blockbuster three-team deal that brought Vin Baker to Seattle. With the stroke of a pen, the most electric partnership of the 1990s was dead.

Neither superstar was ever quite the same without the other. Kemp made one more All-Star appearance in Cleveland before returning from the 1998–99 NBA lockout badly out of shape, losing the transcendent lift that had defined his youth. Payton remained in Seattle for another five and a half seasons as a lone warrior, eventually capturing his elusive championship ring late in his career with the 2006 Miami Heat.

“I watched Shawn go to Cleveland and he got big, and to this day I feel like that was my fault,” Gary Payton admitted with heavy regret. “I didn’t step up the way I should have stepped up with ownership to make them do the right thing for my brother. If we had stayed together in Seattle, we would have run the West for a decade.”

Today, the Seattle SuperSonics no longer exist on the NBA map, relocated to Oklahoma City in 2008. Yet whenever the rain falls over Puget Sound, the memory of green and gold lives on—a blur of No. 20 pointing toward the rafters, and No. 40 rising through the clouds to bring the house down.
